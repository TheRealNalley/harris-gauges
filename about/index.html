How this site works

Sources, definitions and limits · figures below as of Oct 24, 2026 at 10:05 AM CDT

Gulf Coast Gauges republishes stream gauge readings for the Gulf Coast, from the Rio Grande to Apalachicola, as ordinary web pages, one per gauge, and keeps the history the original feeds do not. Every reading since September 11, 2026 is stored: 142,103 so far from 349 sensors on the Harris County Flood Warning System feed, alongside 458 USGS gauges in five states. The pages are rebuilt from those stored readings every 15 minutes. No page is written individually: every number on every page is filled in from a stored reading or an official reference, by the same rules on every page.

This site is not affiliated with any of these agencies, and their numbers are republished as they publish them. When a page shows a reading, it also shows the time that reading was taken, which is not the same as the time the page was built.

What the numbers mean

Flood Warning System gauges: elevation and top of bank

A Flood Warning System gauge, whether Harris County's or another agency's, reports the elevation of the water surface in feet: its height above a fixed reference level, not the depth of the water. That is why a creek can read 102 ft. The number that matters is the distance below top of bank: the gap between the water and the top of the channel at that crossing, using the top-of-bank elevation the gauge's agency publishes with each reading. Top of bank is the agency's reference, not an estimate made here, and it describes the channel at the gauge, not any street or house nearby. Gauges from the other agencies have been recorded here since October 2026, so their history is short; their usual range appears after 14 days.

USGS gauges: gage height and datum

A USGS gauge reports gage height: the water surface above the gauge's own zero point, its datum. For 187 of the 458 USGS gauges here that zero is set at 0 ft NAVD88, so the gage height is also an elevation. The others sit on a local zero; each gauge page says which. None of these numbers is a water depth.

Tide gauges: water level above an ordinary high tide

NOAA tide gauges report the water level relative to mean higher high water (MHHW): the average of the higher of each day's two high tides. 0 ft is an ordinary high tide. The reading is the tide plus whatever is on top of it, such as storm surge or wind pushing water in, and it is set against the NWS's coastal flood thresholds for the station (NOAA's high tide flooding thresholds where the NWS publishes none). The site never shows tide predictions. A few river stations publish no tidal datum and are shown on their own scale.

Usual range

Each gauge's usual range is the middle 80% of its own stored readings (from the 10th to the 90th percentile). It is shaded on the charts so a reading can be read against what is normal for that gauge: tidal and lake gauges sit close to their banks every day, while some creeks are many feet below theirs even after rain. It reflects only the readings stored since September 11, 2026, so it will change as the record grows.

NWS flood stages

For 282 USGS gauges the National Weather Service publishes flood stages: action, minor, moderate and major, in the same feet as the gage height. They are shown only where the NWS's own current reading matched the USGS reading within half a foot when they were fetched, so the two are known to be on the same scale. They are the NWS's references for that location.

Records and annual peaks

USGS keeps the highest gage height of every water year (October to September). The site holds 16,355 such years, but compares today's readings with only 12,970 of them. Many gauges had their datum changed over the years, which moves every reading after it by a fixed amount; years from before a gauge's last datum change, or that USGS marks as measured at another site or datum, are shown on the charts but never ranked against today. "Record" on this site means the highest of the comparable years. The NWS keeps its own list of historic crests, which can differ, and the pages label which is which.

Every gauge in that area that USGS runs and that reports gage height has a page, with one exception. About one site in ten reports two gage heights at once (above and below a structure, or a second sensor on a different zero), and the two can differ by feet. The site stores only the one USGS marks as the primary sensor; where USGS marks more than one, the gauge is left out rather than guessed. Readings at the gauges added in October 2026 start on September 11, 2026, the same day as the rest, filled in from USGS's published 15-minute record.

How a storm is defined

A storm is whatever the National Weather Service declared, not a judgement made here. For Houston–Galveston, every flood alert naming Harris County counts, and since October 24, 2026 so does every coastal flood, storm surge, tropical storm or hurricane watch, warning or advisory; alerts that start within 24 hours of the previous one ending are one event. On a storm with coastal or tropical alerts, the tide gauges are listed first. Each gauge's peak is its highest reading from 12 hours before the first alert to 48 hours after the last one ends, because large channels crest a day or more after the rain. "Came within 2 ft of top of bank" is a fixed threshold, the same for every storm. The storm archive lists 1 event so far, and each new one appears automatically.

Elsewhere on the coast the same rule runs region by region: every flood alert naming any of a region's counties or parishes counts, and each USGS gauge's peak is taken over the same window. NWS alerts there have been recorded in full since October 23, 2026 in Texas and October 23, 2026 in the other states; before that only alerts that also named Harris County were stored, so earlier events appear only when they did.

Peak times are listed as recorded. Their order along a creek reflects where and when rain fell as well as water moving downstream, so the site does not present them as a travel time.

What this site does not do

  • No forecasts. Nothing here predicts where water will go or how high it will get.
  • No safety guidance. The site does not say whether any road, property or route is safe or passable, and a reading below top of bank is not an all-clear.
  • No interpretation of alerts. NWS alerts are shown as issued; for the current list go to alerts.weather.gov, and for decisions about safety use official sources and local emergency management.

Limits worth knowing

  • Gauges fail. A sensor can report late, stop, or report in error, and nothing here verifies readings independently. Pages say when a gauge's latest reading is more than a day old.
  • County gauges report when the water changes, not on a timer: anywhere from every 15 minutes to every few hours. A gap in a chart is normal.
  • Some sites are left out. Spillway, dam, gate and levee sites are not shown, because their top-of-bank figure is not a channel bank.
  • History is short for county gauges. Their record here starts on September 11, 2026; USGS gauges carry decades.
  • Recent USGS readings are provisional and can be revised after review.
  • Times are local: Central everywhere except the Apalachicola and Tallahassee region of Florida, which is Eastern. Every time is labelled (CDT, CST, EDT or EST).

Contact

To report a reading that looks wrong, a page that is broken, or a question about the data or how it is shown: contact [at] gulfcoastgauges.com. This site can't answer questions about conditions at a particular property or road; for those, use the official sources above and local emergency management.

Using the data

Every gauge page has two CSV downloads: every reading from the last 90 days, and the daily low and high for the gauge's whole record here. Every storm page has a CSV of each gauge's peak. The site stores every reading it has collected; the downloads stop at 90 days to keep the site within its hosting limits. Any gauge can be put on another website with the embed widget. If you use the numbers, credit the agency that measured them (Harris County Flood Control District, USGS or NWS) and check its terms; this site only republishes them.
